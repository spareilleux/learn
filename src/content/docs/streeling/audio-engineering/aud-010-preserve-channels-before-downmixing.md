## The project question

A GA reviewer receives a stereo guitar reference and a mono synthesis. Averaging the reference before checking its channels can erase the very signal being compared. This lesson builds an explicit channel matrix, keeps the original support and identifies what cannot be recovered after reduction. The task concerns data preservation, with no new recording or listening judgment.

## 1. Exercise 1

Declare left samples `[1,-1,1,-1]` and right samples `[-1,1,-1,1]`, each on the same four-frame support. Each channel has squared-sample sum 4. A frame is a simultaneous collection of channel samples: these are four stereo frames and eight scalar samples. Losing this distinction can corrupt both duration and byte-count reasoning.

How many frames and scalar samples are present, and what is each channel's energy?

> **Answer.** There are 4 frames and 8 scalar samples. Each channel's squared-sample sum is 4; this is not a calibrated energy in joules.

## 2. Exercise 2

Use the declared reduction `m[n]=(L[n]+R[n])/2`. Every output sample is zero because the channel values have opposite signs. The channel input is nonzero. A silent reduced buffer therefore cannot establish silent original channels, and a mono null test cannot certify equality of the two original stereo records.

Calculate the mono vector and explain why a silence label would be misleading.

> **Answer.** The mono vector is `[0,0,0,0]`, with sum of squares 0. Cancellation under the chosen matrix explains that result; the original channels remain nonzero.

## 3. Exercise 3

Keep a second control with identical channels: `L=R=[1,-1,1,-1]`. Averaging now preserves that vector and its squared-sample sum. The contrast isolates interchannel sign as the changed condition. It does not establish how every real microphone pair behaves or justify interpreting opposite signs as a wiring defect without acquisition context.

What does the identical-channel control produce, and what variable does it isolate?

> **Answer.** Mono is `[1,-1,1,-1]` with squared-sample sum 4. The control changes the relation between channels; it does not diagnose an actual recording setup.

## 4. Exercise 4

The mono output alone cannot distinguish opposite nonzero channels from two zero channels. This is a concrete many-to-one map. Preserve channel order, sample rate, original bytes and matrix before publishing a reduced comparison. A later gain adjustment cannot recover a discarded channel difference; retaining provenance is necessary for another reviewer to repeat the transformation.

Can an inverse operation recover the two inputs uniquely from the zero mono result?

> **Answer.** No. Opposite channels and two zero channels share the same output. Retain both originals and the declared matrix; the reduced buffer alone is insufficient.

## 5. Exercise 5

Read the retained runner's mono contract before suggesting stereo support. Its current teaching buffer counts describe that contract, not this new stereo example. The W3C speaker-mixing reference specifies an averaging rule for stereo reduction; selecting a rule still requires documenting channel interpretation. An agent's proposed report must distinguish a source observation from a newly executed decoder result.

What would falsify a report claiming that zero mono proves zero input?

> **Answer.** The authored opposite-channel case already falsifies it. The report must preserve per-channel values and acknowledge that the selected runner was read, not rerun or extended.
