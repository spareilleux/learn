A guitarist can ask GA for a chord whose voices sound together. Individually valid samples do not guarantee that their sum fits a normalized output. The dangerous shortcut is to estimate headroom as though all voices were unrelated noise. Shared attacks, copied excitation or a deliberate unison may instead align. This lesson uses tiny signed buffers so the learner can inspect every addition and distinguish an upstream mix failure from a final output clamp.

## Read the retained evidence

Read the retained runner's normalized-sample guard and integer WAV conversion. OpenStax describes superposition as adding displacements, with constructive and destructive interference depending on relative phase. Our discrete arrays apply linear addition as an authored signal model, without claiming measured guitar phase relationships.

### Exercise 1

The two input buffers are both `[0.8,-0.8,0.4]`. Each input peak is `0.8`. Add samples with matching indices to obtain `[1.6,-1.6,0.8]`. Opposite signs within a waveform do not help when the second source repeats those same signs at the same times. An absolute peak checks the largest magnitude, including a negative excursion.

What is the raw sum and its peak?

<details><summary>Answer 1</summary>

The sum is `[1.6,-1.6,0.8]`; its peak magnitude is `1.6`. Each source was individually within the limit, while their correlated sum exceeds `1`.

</details>

### Exercise 2

Apply the stipulated gain `0.5` to the raw sum before enforcing the final limit. The result returns to `[0.8,-0.8,0.4]`, with peak `0.8`. This operation trades output amplitude for headroom. It does not recover two independent musical voices from their sum, and it should not be described as an automatic loudness match.

Which gain is used and what buffer reaches the output?

<details><summary>Answer 2</summary>

Gain `0.5` gives `[0.8,-0.8,0.4]` and peak `0.8`. The gain is explicit preparation, not evidence of equal perceived loudness.

</details>

### Exercise 3

Now reverse the operation order. Clipping the raw sum to `[-1,1]` produces `[1,-1,0.8]`. Scaling that clipped buffer by `0.5` would produce `[0.5,-0.5,0.4]`, which differs from the unclipped mix. Reducing gain later cannot reconstruct peak values that were already flattened. Preserve an internal probe when diagnosing such a boundary.

Why is clipping followed by gain a different transformation?

<details><summary>Answer 3</summary>

The clipped raw array is `[1,-1,0.8]`, so later scaling gives `[0.5,-0.5,0.4]`. The earlier extreme values were lost; a small final peak does not prove an undistorted internal mix.

</details>

### Exercise 4

For the negative control, replace the second buffer with `[-0.8,0.8,-0.4]` while keeping gain `0.5`. The raw and scaled sums are `[0,0,0]`. This does not mean either source is silent. It shows why phase relation belongs to the input description, and why a gain justified only by this cancelling example would be fragile.

What does the control establish about universal headroom estimates?

<details><summary>Answer 4</summary>

Its peak is `0`, despite nonzero inputs. Cancellation and correlated reinforcement give different peaks, so neither example alone supplies a universal source-independent estimate.

</details>

### Exercise 5

A practical chord test should include aligned attacks, unisons and deliberately opposing signals before claiming a general mix margin. Keep the raw sum, gain position and clipping policy visible in the review. A limiter is another nonlinear operation requiring its own evidence; naming one does not replace the check. No listener preference follows from the finite arrays.

What observation would reject a claim that every intermediate stayed within range?

<details><summary>Answer 5</summary>

A retained pre-clamp sample with magnitude above `1` rejects it. A legal final WAV alone cannot establish that stronger internal claim, because clipping may have hidden the excursion.

</details>
