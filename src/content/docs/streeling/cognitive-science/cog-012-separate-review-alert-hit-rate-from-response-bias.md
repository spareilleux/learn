> This lesson uses an authored fixed case and inspected sources. Counts, times, labels and signals are invented teaching inputs. Human technical review, fluent-language review, actual learner attempts, runtime execution, production adoption and publication decisions remain undecided.

A review assistant finds nearly every planted defect and is praised as highly sensitive. It may also flag almost every clean item. The agreement prerequisite compares raters without deciding which one is right. This lesson supplies an explicit external defect key and asks how each response behaves conditional on that key. Matching another rater and detecting a real defect are different endpoints.

Place true defect status in rows and the reviewer’s alert decision in columns, following the official confusion-matrix definition. A hit belongs to the defect row, while a false alarm belongs to the clean row. Their denominators must therefore be different. Overall alert frequency instead mixes both rows and depends on how many defective items the exercise contains. Calling alert frequency sensitivity would hide that dependence.

We use the difference between hit and false-alarm rates as a declared descriptive separation measure. It is not Gaussian signal-detection sensitivity and does not identify a psychological mechanism. A tendency to say yes can change both rates. The always-alert control is especially useful because its perfect hit rate coexists with no separation under our definition. This exposes what the headline leaves out without requiring a distributional model.

The retained GA observation file says a displayed confidence is a retrieval score and that the selected data contain no learner answer. It cannot supply the external defect key or real review responses used here. A future dogfood task must retain clean as well as defective items, the adjudicated key, original alerts and assistance history. First calculate the table manually; then challenge any agent summary that promotes the hit rate alone into evidence of better judgment.

### Exercise 1

Calculate the hit and false-alarm rates with their own denominators.

<details><summary>Answer 1</summary>

Hit rate is `8/10=0.8`; false-alarm rate is `4/10=0.4`. Both condition on actual status. Dividing both counts by the entire collection would answer a different joint-frequency question.

</details>

### Exercise 2

Compute alert frequency, accuracy and the declared separation measure.

<details><summary>Answer 2</summary>

They are `0.6`, `0.7` and `0.4`. Alert frequency counts decisions to flag; accuracy counts matches to the key; separation contrasts conditional rates. None is a measured psychological trait.

</details>

### Exercise 3

Apply the always-alert control. What happens to the apparently impressive hit rate?

<details><summary>Answer 3</summary>

Hit rate becomes `1`, but false-alarm rate also becomes `1`, accuracy is `0.5` and separation is `0`. Perfect hits alone do not demonstrate discrimination when all clean items are flagged.

</details>

### Exercise 4

Would the prerequisite’s rater agreement table supply the same conclusion?

<details><summary>Answer 4</summary>

No. Agreement needs paired rater labels, while this calculation needs an external correctness key. Two raters can agree on the wrong alert, and an adjudicated key can itself require independent validation.

</details>

### Exercise 5

What must a real review study retain before discussing response bias?

<details><summary>Answer 5</summary>

Retain defect status, original alerts, item mix, key construction, task instructions and any assistance. These rates motivate a response-pattern question; they do not diagnose cognition or establish a production improvement from invented counts.

</details>
