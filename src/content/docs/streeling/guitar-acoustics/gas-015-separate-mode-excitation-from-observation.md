A missing spectral component need not mean the string was never excited in that mode. A point observer placed at a node cannot see its displacement. [GA](https://github.com/GuitarAlchemist/ga) explanations should preserve the distinction between excitation, propagation and observation before diagnosing a guitar model. Here the modal amplitudes remain fixed while the observer moves.

## Ask which stage can explain a missing component

The excitation coefficient describes the prepared modal state, whereas the observation weight describes how one coordinate contributes to the output. Their product can vanish because either factor vanishes. Seeing only the product cannot identify which factor caused the zero. This simple factorization is useful when an agent is tempted to adjust a pluck parameter after finding a spectral dip in an observed signal.

A point at an ideal node is especially informative because its zero does not depend on the mode’s amplitude. Increasing excitation cannot make displacement at that exact node reveal the mode. Moving the observer can help, provided its new position and the same excitation are retained. The control demonstrates that idea without claiming complete recovery: a mode that is invisible at both selected positions still needs another observation.

Real guitar outputs usually apply additional transfer functions. A magnetic pickup may respond to motion in a way that requires velocity, field geometry and finite aperture, while a microphone observes radiated pressure after body and room effects. Our point-displacement observer deliberately supplies none of those mechanisms. A useful next experiment would characterize the observation path before attributing a missing peak to synthesis. The direct modal table is cheaper and more interpretable than adding a compensating agent rule whose cause has not been identified.

### Exercise 1

Apply the displayed operator at the main position. Which listed modes are invisible?

<details><summary>Answer 1</summary>

The zero-mode list gives the even modes in this finite example. Their authored excitation coefficients are nonzero, so observation alone cannot prove absence of excitation.

</details>

### Exercise 2

Interpret the negative observed coefficient. Is it negative loudness?

<details><summary>Answer 2</summary>

It is a signed displacement coefficient relative to the declared modal phase. Loudness is not a signed displacement and was not measured.

</details>

### Exercise 3

Can the main observation uniquely recover all the original amplitudes?

<details><summary>Answer 3</summary>

No. Multiplication by a zero observation weight erases that mode’s amplitude. Many excitation arrays give the same observation at those indices.

</details>

### Exercise 4

Move to the control position. Which formerly invisible mode becomes available?

<details><summary>Answer 4</summary>

The second mode becomes nonzero while the fourth remains invisible. The excitation was unchanged; the changed observation explains the new coefficient.

</details>

### Exercise 5

What rejects a claim that a missing observed mode proves zero excitation?

<details><summary>Answer 5</summary>

The main case itself supplies a nonzero excitation coefficient with zero observed displacement. A real diagnosis needs a known observation transfer and additional positions or measurements.

</details>
