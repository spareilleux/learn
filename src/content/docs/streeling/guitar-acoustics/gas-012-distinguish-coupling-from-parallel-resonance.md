A body-like resonance in a guitar render does not by itself show that energy can travel back from the body into a string. The distinction matters when a future GA lesson or control promises sympathetic response. We build a symmetric mechanical analogue whose equations contain an explicit coupling term. Its modal split is independently calculable. The analogue explains the required relationship between coordinates; it does not claim that an existing engine has implemented the relationship or matches an acoustic guitar.

### Exercise 1

Let each mass be `1 kg`, each anchor stiffness `100 N/m`, and the connecting stiffness `150 N/m`. With displacements `x1` and `x2`, the first equation is `m*x1''=-k*x1-kc*(x1-x2)`; the second exchanges the coordinates. The connecting force depends on their difference, so specifying two separate resonance frequencies would not specify this model.

What physical relation makes the model coupled?

<details><summary>Answer 1</summary>

The force on each mass includes the other displacement through `kc*(x1-x2)`. Both directions appear; a downstream sum alone does not express that relationship.

</details>

### Exercise 2

For motion with `x1=x2`, the connecting spring does not stretch. The angular frequency is `sqrt(k/m)=10 rad/s`, corresponding to `10/(2*pi) Hz`. Keeping angular and ordinary frequency separate prevents a factor error. The result follows from symmetry and the authored parameters, without identifying any actual guitar's lowest body mode.

What is the same-direction mode, and why does coupling not raise it?

<details><summary>Answer 2</summary>

Its angular frequency is `10 rad/s`, or `10/(2*pi) Hz`. Equal displacement leaves the connecting spring unchanged, so only the anchor stiffness contributes.

</details>

### Exercise 3

For `x1=-x2`, the connecting spring extends by twice either displacement. The effective stiffness becomes `k+2*kc`, giving `sqrt((k+2*kc)/m)=20 rad/s`, or `20/(2*pi) Hz`. This mode distinguishes reciprocal coupling from two independent identical oscillators. The split depends on the assumed symmetry; unequal masses require another calculation.

Calculate the opposite-direction mode and identify the added stiffness.

<details><summary>Answer 3</summary>

It is `20 rad/s`, or `20/(2*pi) Hz`. The added stiffness is `2*kc`, because opposite displacements change the connecting spring by twice one coordinate.

</details>

### Exercise 4

The negative control sets coupling stiffness to `0 N/m` while preserving the masses and anchors. Both mode frequencies become `10 rad/s`. Each oscillator still has an anchored oscillation, but the frequency split disappears. This falsifies any explanation that attributes the split solely to the unchanged anchor stiffness or merely to observing two coordinates.

Does zero coupling imply silence?

<details><summary>Answer 4</summary>

No. Both modes are `10 rad/s`; independent oscillation remains possible. The control removes the coupling-induced split, not all motion or every source of sound.

</details>

### Exercise 5

A source review should locate the variable that one vibrating component passes back to another before claiming sympathetic exchange. A body filter driven by a mixed string output may color the result without providing that feedback path. Conversely, one excerpt cannot prove a repository-wide absence. Retain the precise render path and initial conditions, then request an actual intervention if the claim concerns production behavior.

What observation would challenge a coupling explanation for an engine?

<details><summary>Answer 5</summary>

If changing the alleged coupling leaves the relevant state interaction and modal prediction unchanged under a controlled probe, the claimed mechanism lacks support. Our algebra is a proposed diagnostic, not that executed probe.

</details>
