## Project question

A chased metal strip in a proposed sculpture has a visible gap beside a fixed stop. An agent might preserve that gap in a mesh while forgetting the temperature at which it was specified. This lesson adds a thermal reference to the static-clearance prerequisite. We calculate free expansion with an explicitly invented coefficient, then distinguish a negative predicted gap from a measured failure of a real joint.

## Read the retained boundary

Atlas creates metallic and roughness settings in a material helper. Those appearance parameters do not specify an alloy or thermal expansion coefficient. The missing physical property must remain an authored input rather than being inferred from a shiny render.

## Interpret the model

The OpenStax section states a linear expansion approximation. Here length is measured at the reference temperature, temperature change is a difference in kelvins, and the coefficient has reciprocal-kelvin units. The strip is free to grow toward a stop that does not move. This deliberately excludes restraint forces. Subtract growth from the original gap; do not subtract an absolute temperature. A visual material can represent metal without supplying any mechanical constitutive law. The negative control changes the temperature difference alone, exposing why an unchanged mesh does not guarantee an unchanged clearance under a different physical interpretation.

The direct baseline is a labelled sketch and a hand calculation with the answer panels closed. Preserve intermediate values and units, then apply the changed input in the negative control. A disagreement is useful evidence: locate whether it comes from the definition, arithmetic, representation or intended task before changing the model. Agreement on the main case alone does not establish a universal rule. These finite examples deliberately expose an assumption that a smooth preview could conceal.

## Corrected exercises

### Exercise 1

Compute the free growth of the authored strip. Which units cancel?

<details><summary>Answer 1</summary>

Growth is `0.2 mm`. Reciprocal kelvins cancel kelvins, leaving the length unit. The coefficient is an input, not an estimate obtained from the material’s appearance.

</details>

### Exercise 2

What gap remains, and what narrow conclusion follows?

<details><summary>Answer 2</summary>

The remaining gap is `0.1 mm`. The linear free-growth model predicts separation for this input; no real strip or contact was observed.

</details>

### Exercise 3

Apply the larger-temperature negative control. What does a negative gap mean here?

<details><summary>Answer 3</summary>

Growth is `0.4 mm` and remaining gap is `-0.1 mm`. The free-growth geometry overlaps the stop; the model does not calculate the restrained stress.

</details>

### Exercise 4

Could a metallic shader parameter replace the expansion coefficient?

<details><summary>Answer 4</summary>

No. Appearance parameters control a visual material. They do not identify alloy, temperature dependence, restraint or the physical coefficient required by this calculation.

</details>

### Exercise 5

What would falsify transferring this constant-coefficient model to a particular craft piece?

<details><summary>Answer 5</summary>

A relevant measured expansion outside the declared error allowance, a moving stop, strong thermal gradients or constrained growth would invalidate at least one premise. Retain the discrepancy rather than claiming certification.

</details>
