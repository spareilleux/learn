A guitarist may seem to recognize an interval while actually recognizing the sound assigned to it. If every major-third item uses one guitar timbre and every fourth another, the answer can be guessed without comparing pitches. GA should expose that design flaw before reporting a listening skill. We build a small crossed stimulus plan. Its cell coverage can be checked without producing audio, while its musical and learner validity remain separate questions.

## Read the retained evidence

Read the retained practice packet's cue policies: different target tasks deliberately reveal or hide different labels. That principle applies to unintended sound cues as well as text. NIST defines a full factorial design by crossing every setting of each factor with every setting of the others. Our lesson uses that combinatorial property; it does not borrow an experimental result or imply that trial order was randomized.

### Exercise 1

Declare interval labels `[M3,P4]` and timbre labels `[nylon,steel]`. Their Cartesian product has `4` cells, ordered by interval then timbre. This is a design table, not a claim that the two synthesized timbres are perceptually distinct or that the selected intervals are equally difficult. Those are further questions requiring actual stimuli and listeners.

List the cells in the declared order.

<details><summary>Answer 1</summary>

They are `[(M3,nylon),(M3,steel),(P4,nylon),(P4,steel)]`. The order binds later count vectors to their conditions.

</details>

### Exercise 2

Assign `2` trials to each cell. The count vector is `[2,2,2,2]`, totaling `8`. Each interval now appears with each timbre, so timbre no longer perfectly determines the interval label from the assignment alone. This removes that deterministic shortcut in the table, without guaranteeing the absence of every other cue.

What are total trials and covered cells?

<details><summary>Answer 2</summary>

There are `8` trials and `4` covered cells. All cells have equal positive counts, so this authored assignment is complete and balanced.

</details>

### Exercise 3

Balance does not specify playback level, register, direction, duration or answer visibility. Any of these can become another cue. State which dimensions the task holds fixed and which it varies, then retain the actual stimulus identity. A future renderer that ignores the timbre choice would fail a different fidelity check even though the planned table is balanced.

Does cell balance alone validate a listening experiment?

<details><summary>Answer 3</summary>

No. The table specifies assignment coverage. Actual stimuli, cue control, playback and real answers still need observation; neither learning nor perceptual equivalence follows from the counts.

</details>

### Exercise 4

The negative control assigns `4` trials to `(M3,nylon)` and `4` to `(P4,steel)`, with no other cells. Its vector is `[4,0,0,4]`. It still has `8` trials, and each individual factor has balanced marginal counts. Yet only `2` cells are covered, allowing timbre to identify the answer perfectly in this authored mapping.

Why do equal marginals miss the confound?

<details><summary>Answer 4</summary>

They ignore pairings. The control covers only `2` of the `4` cells, so the interval label and timbre remain perfectly linked despite equal marginal totals.

</details>

### Exercise 5

A source-assisted agent can check the table and preserve a randomized-order seed if a later experiment actually uses one. It should not fabricate a shuffled order, playback success or participant responses. Before interpreting a score, compare the retained played stimulus against its intended cell, keep assistance visible and specify an assessment with novel examples if transfer is the aim.

What would falsify a claim that the design crosses both factors completely?

<details><summary>Answer 5</summary>

Any required cell with zero trials falsifies completeness. The control provides that witness, while a production claim would additionally require evidence that the intended stimuli were actually played.

</details>
