A catalog pitch-class set can answer which tones appear somewhere, while failing to answer which are allowed on a particular melodic direction. An agent that replaces two directional inventories with their union can accept a phrase the original model excludes. We construct that loss explicitly for a future GA explanation or grammar proposal. The numbers are authored teaching material; they do not describe a named tradition or authorize conclusions about anyone's performance practice.

## Read the retained evidence

Read the retained intonation packet's catalog observations and its separate constructed-frequency fields. The catalog summary is a selected pitch-class representation, not a full melodic grammar. The prerequisite resolves a degree against a declared scale form. Our additional input is direction. We examine a precise finite membership policy, rather than infer that every traditional melody can be described by two unordered sets.

### Exercise 1

The ascending inventory is `[0,2,4,7,9]`; the descending inventory is `[0,2,3,7,9]`. Both are offsets in one declared register. Their union is `[0,2,3,4,7,9]`, but that union has no field saying where `3` or `4` was permitted. Keeping provenance separate prevents an authored contrast from becoming a mislabeled ethnomusicological claim.

Which information does the union erase?

<details><summary>Answer 1</summary>

It erases that `4` belongs only to the ascending inventory and `3` only to the descending one. The union preserves membership somewhere, not membership by direction.

</details>

### Exercise 2

Use phrase `[0,4,7,4,0]`. Determine direction from each adjacent pair by comparing target and source, then test the target against the corresponding inventory. The first two moves rise, and their targets are allowed. This policy does not constrain jump size or require neighboring scale degrees; it is intentionally narrower than a complete melodic grammar.

What are the first two decisions under this rule?

<details><summary>Answer 2</summary>

They are `[true,true]`: targets `4` and `7` belong to the ascending inventory. This says nothing about their stylistic suitability or physical execution.

</details>

### Exercise 3

The move from `7` to `4` descends, and `4` is absent from the descending inventory. The final move to `0` is allowed. Thus the complete decision vector is `[true,true,false,true]`, and phrase acceptance is false. The first failing position is a useful witness that a reviewer can inspect without running a music engine.

Why does the original authored policy reject the phrase?

<details><summary>Answer 3</summary>

Its descending move to `4` fails membership. The decision vector is `[true,true,false,true]`, so not all required steps are allowed.

</details>

### Exercise 4

For the negative control, replace both inventories by `[0,2,3,4,7,9]`, their union. The same phrase now gives `[true,true,true,true]` and is accepted. The union itself is unchanged between descriptions, so a test checking only that projection cannot distinguish the original policy from its lossy replacement.

What does the control prove about using a union as a complete policy?

<details><summary>Answer 4</summary>

It supplies a phrase accepted after projection but rejected before it. Equal unions cannot establish equivalent directional acceptance rules.

</details>

### Exercise 5

A real musical account might also depend on register, characteristic phrases, context, intonation and source authority. Our small policy intentionally omits those dimensions. A future DSL should first reproduce the manual witness and retain unknowns rather than promoting the simple sets to a normative tradition model. A parser accepting the data would not establish musical validity.

What would falsify a handoff claiming to preserve this directional policy?

<details><summary>Answer 5</summary>

If the receiver accepts the main phrase solely from the union while claiming equivalence to the original inventories, the witness contradicts that claim. Broader cultural interpretation still needs appropriate sources and musician review.

</details>
