## The project question

Which visual policy should an Observatory agent choose when host capacity, competing workloads and adapter availability can change? [RAND's Robust Decision Making page](https://www.rand.org/pubs/tools/TL320/tool/robust-decision-making.html) treats disagreement about system relationships, probabilities and values as distinct sources of uncertainty. It describes framing, evaluation across futures, vulnerability analysis and trade-off analysis. We practice a small decision calculation; this is not a complete implementation of that workflow.

The [historical BRUTAL coexistence report](https://github.com/GuitarAlchemist/ga/blob/76efbffd5af82b46c3146957a6bd4504008eeb89/Experiments/Brutal/evidence/m3-20261020-000727.md) contains variation even without the additional rendering workload. The earlier [analysis in PM-005](../../product-management/pm-005-interpret-brutal-coexistence-experiments/) retains its inconclusive follow-up. Those observations motivate examining alternatives. They do not supply the losses or probabilities in this lesson's table, or validate an adaptive renderer.

## 1. Frame the decision before assigning probabilities

Use three hypothetical strategies: coexistence-first `C`, detail-first `P` and adaptive-candidate `A`. Use three hypothetical conditions: limited host, capable host and contended host. Each cell is an authored loss score; lower is preferred. Assume the points have comparable cardinal meaning across rows and columns. This assumption itself needs justification before a real decision.

| Strategy | Limited host | Capable host | Contended host |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| C | 3 | 6 | 6 |
| P | 8 | 0 | 14 |
| A | 4 | 3 | 5 |

The labels do not certify installed modes or the behavior of the controller in [CYB-005](../../cybernetics/cyb-005-stop-render-policy-flapping/). There is no sampling distribution or measured frequency of these conditions. Listing three scenarios does not assign each probability one third.

### Practice exercise 1: identify the unsupported inference

An agent calls A's loss of 5 in the contended-host column a measured GPU penalty and assigns all three columns equal probability. Which assumptions did it add?

> **Answer:** It converted an authored loss-point premise into a hardware measurement and introduced probabilities absent from the table. Neither follows from listing scenarios. A real evaluation needs measured or explicitly modeled consequences, justified value scales and a separately stated treatment of uncertainty.

## 2. Calculate regret relative to each scenario's best

For each column, first find the lowest loss attainable by any listed strategy. The best losses are `[3,0,5]`. Define a strategy's regret in that column as its loss minus that best loss. The calculation asks how far it falls behind the listed alternative one could select with knowledge of that condition.

| Strategy | Limited-host regret | Capable-host regret | Contended-host regret | Maximum regret |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| C | 0 | 6 | 1 | 6 |
| P | 5 | 0 | 9 | 9 |
| A | 1 | 3 | 0 | 3 |

Minimizing the largest regret selects A on this table. Its maximum regret of 3 is a loss-point difference, not a probability, frame time, universal guarantee or proof that adaptation is best. The criterion compares the declared alternatives over the declared scenario set.

### Practice exercise 2: reconstruct the criterion

Derive P's regret in the contended-host case and all three maximum regrets. Which strategy minimizes maximum regret, and what is the unit of the result?

> **Answer:** P's contended-host regret is `14-5=9`. Maximum regrets are C 6, P 9 and A 3, so A is selected. The numbers are differences in the table's assumed comparable loss points. They are not measured rendering milliseconds or failure probabilities.

## 3. Compare an explicitly probabilistic decision

Now assume probabilities `[0.1,0.8,0.1]` for the same three columns. Expected loss is the weighted sum of each row. C gives 5.7, P gives 2.2 and A gives 3.3; minimizing expected loss selects P. Different criteria and premises can produce different choices without an arithmetic contradiction.

These probabilities are stipulated, not inferred from the historical report. Expected-loss reasoning becomes useful when a distribution is justified for the question and consequences are represented appropriately. A minimax-regret calculation does not remove the need to defend its own loss scale, scenario set or treatment of worst cases.

### Practice exercise 3: explain the changed choice

Compute P's expected loss under the stated probabilities. Why can it beat A under expected loss while losing under maximum regret?

> **Answer:** `0.1*8+0.8*0+0.1*14=2.2`, below A's 3.3. The assumed distribution places most weight on P's favorable capable-host column. Maximum regret instead looks at the largest column-relative shortfall, where P has 9 and A 3. The choices depend on different stated premises and criteria.

## 4. Find the belief that changes the comparison

Let the capable-host probability be p and allocate each other condition probability `(1-p)/2`. Expected losses become `4.5+1.5*p` for C, `11-11*p` for P and `4.5-1.5*p` for A. Equating P and A gives `p=13/19`, approximately 0.684210526. Above that value P has lower expected loss; below it A has lower expected loss in this pairwise comparison.

This crossover makes a disagreement inspectable: how much weight would the decision need to place on the favorable scenario? It does not estimate that weight. At p equal to zero, C and A tie in expected loss; the algebra should retain ties rather than invent a unique winner.

### Practice exercise 4: derive the crossover

Solve `11-11*p=4.5-1.5*p`. Is the result evidence that the capable-host scenario will occur with that probability?

> **Answer:** Rearranging gives `6.5=9.5*p`, so p is `13/19`, approximately 0.684210526. It is a conditional decision boundary under the authored losses and probability allocation. It is not a forecast, observed frequency or posterior probability of a future host condition.

## 5. Search for an omitted failure condition

Add an adapter-unavailable column with losses C 4, P 10 and A 20. Its best loss is 4, so its regrets are C 0, P 6 and A 16. Across all four columns, maximum regrets become C 6, P 9 and A 16. The minimax-regret choice changes to C.

The strongest counterargument to the original choice is therefore concrete: the small table omitted a condition where the adaptive candidate cannot perform its role. The new column is also invented; it identifies a modeling vulnerability rather than proving a real adapter failure. A simpler alternative to switching policy is retaining a fixed setting while collecting bounded, relevant evidence.

### Practice exercise 5: write the next investigation

After adding the fourth scenario, which strategy minimizes maximum regret? State a trigger for revisiting the decision and a result that would reject the adaptive candidate without claiming every future has been covered.

> **Answer:** C is selected with maximum regret 6, compared with P 9 and A 16. Revisit when actual adapter availability, representative workload measurements or stakeholder objectives change. Reject the candidate for the intended use if it cannot operate in a required condition or violates a declared quality or adjacent-task limit. Test those requirements directly; this finite table cannot certify robustness to every future.
