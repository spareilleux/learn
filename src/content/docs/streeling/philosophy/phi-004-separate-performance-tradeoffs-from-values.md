## The project question

An Observatory agent says its fastest or most detailed rendering policy is “best.” Best for whom, under which priorities and constraints? The [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's Decision Theory entry](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/decision-theory/) distinguishes the beliefs and values involved in choice and discusses normative decision criteria. A numerical ranking needs premises about what matters as well as consequences. This lesson makes one such premise visible; it does not adopt expected utility as a complete ethical theory.

The [historical BRUTAL evaluation](https://github.com/GuitarAlchemist/ga/blob/76efbffd5af82b46c3146957a6bd4504008eeb89/Experiments/Brutal/README.md) motivates the question because an additional renderer and a neighboring interactive task share resources. Source and historical report identity do not determine how their users' interests should be weighted. Our examples below are independently authored, not extracted image-quality ratings or measured penalties.

## 1. State attributes before collapsing them into a score

Consider four hypothetical settings. D is a constructed detail score in points, with higher preferred. O is constructed overhead added to an adjacent task, in milliseconds, with lower preferred. Assume these attributes summarize what this calculation considers; they need not capture everything a real user values.

| Setting | D: detail points | O: adjacent-task overhead in ms |
| --- | --- | --- |
| L | 70 | 2 |
| B | 85 | 5 |
| H | 95 | 12 |
| W | 70 | 8 |

There are two distinct questions. Empirical work would ask which consequences actually follow from a setting under declared conditions. Value reasoning would ask which consequences are acceptable or preferred, and whose interests the decision represents. A future measurement can improve the first answer without settling the second.

### Practice exercise 1: expose the missing premise

An agent selects H solely because 95 is the largest detail score. What preference has it assumed, and which kind of evidence is still absent from this table?

> **Answer:** It has treated detail as sufficient to determine the choice, overlooking the adjacent-task cost and other possible constraints. The table also contains authored attributes, not actual measured consequences or elicited preferences. Even accurate measurements would not by themselves establish whose priorities should govern.

## 2. Use dominance to narrow a partial ordering

Within these two attributes, one option dominates another when it offers at least as much detail and no more overhead, with a strict improvement in at least one attribute. L dominates W: both have 70 detail points, while overhead is 2 rather than 8 ms. B also dominates W, offering 85 points at 5 ms. L, B and H remain nondominated because gaining detail costs additional overhead.

![Hypothetical detail and adjacent-task overhead options](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/spareilleux/learn/218a2bffc18fe2251956197e882cb6c7ea5d7962/code/streeling/evidence/observatory-decisions-2026-10-22/two-attribute-options.png)

The plot shows the authored options, with higher D and lower O preferred. Its dashed boundary at O equal to 6 ms will serve a later hypothetical constraint. Dominance does not supply a total ranking of the remaining options, and an omitted attribute could change the comparison with W.

### Practice exercise 2: distinguish elimination from selection

Which options dominate W, and which options are nondominated? Does that result uniquely select a setting for a real user?

> **Answer:** L and B dominate W under the two stated attributes. L, B and H are nondominated. This narrows the options but does not choose uniquely among them or establish real user preferences. The conclusion is limited to the declared attributes and their authored values.

## 3. Make a trade-off weight dimensionally explicit

Assume a linear score `U=D-lambda*O`. Here lambda has units **detail points per millisecond**. This assumes a constant exchange rate and treats D as a cardinal scale on which the arithmetic is meaningful. A numbered ordinal rating alone would not justify that assumption.

| Weight in points per ms | L score | B score | H score | W score | Highest score |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0.5 | 69 | 82.5 | 89 | 66 | H |
| 2 | 66 | 75 | 71 | 54 | B |
| 6 | 58 | 55 | 23 | 22 | L |

Nothing in the attribute table selects one of these weights. Under the assumed rule, H and B tie at lambda `10/7`; B and L tie at lambda 5. These boundaries describe the stated model, not objectively correct priorities.

### Practice exercise 3: reveal a unit error

At lambda 2 points per millisecond, B wins. The overhead values are converted into seconds. Which weight preserves the ranking? What happens if the agent keeps the numeric weight 2 without changing its unit?

> **Answer:** Use 2000 points per second to preserve the same trade-off and B's score of 75. Keeping 2 points per second makes the penalty a thousand times smaller and selects H instead. That is a changed value model caused by a unit error, not new evidence that users prefer H.

## 4. Keep a hard constraint outside the compensatory score

Now impose an authored limit: overhead must be at most 6 ms. L and B are feasible; H and W are excluded before scoring. With weight 0.5, the unconstrained highest score is H, but the highest feasible score is B. A large detail gain cannot compensate for crossing a hard constraint under this decision rule.

The limit is a new value premise. It is not a threshold from the source controller or an existing product obligation. [CYB-005](../../cybernetics/cyb-005-stop-render-policy-flapping/) uses a different quantity, GPU frame time; confusing that sensor with added adjacent-task overhead would mix both units of meaning and decision purpose.

### Practice exercise 4: apply feasibility before ranking

Under the hypothetical 6 ms hard limit and weight 0.5, which setting is selected? May an agent restore H by silently averaging its violation with other successful cases?

> **Answer:** B is selected among feasible L and B. H violates the hard limit and cannot re-enter through its higher score or an average of other cases under the stated rule. Revising the constraint would require an explicit justified change to the decision premises, not a hidden arithmetic adjustment.

## 5. Distinguish a transparent model from a legitimate decision

An explicit weight makes a trade-off inspectable. It does not show that affected users agreed to it, that a score covers accessibility or privacy, or that the agent has authority to impose the result. Optimizing an individual preference is not automatically an acceptable allocation of a shared machine. Conversely, another stakeholder's objection is not merely measurement noise to smooth away.

The strongest counterargument to this simple model is its assumption that all relevant interests can be represented by two cardinal attributes and one constant exchange rate. A simpler practical alternative is to ask for an acceptable operating range, compare a small set of feasible options and keep a reversible choice while collecting relevant evidence. Reject the linear model if omitted concerns or observed choices systematically contradict the ranking it is supposed to represent.

### Practice exercise 5: write a justified recommendation

An agent reports “the benchmark proves B is ethically correct.” Replace that claim with a recommendation that states its empirical, value and authority limits.

> **Answer:** Under the authored attributes, the assumed linear score with weight 2 selects B; under the authored 6 ms constraint, B is also the best feasible option at weight 0.5. These calculations establish neither actual benchmark outcomes nor an ethical mandate. A real recommendation needs measured consequences, stated stakeholder priorities and constraints, consideration of omitted interests and appropriate authority for the change.
