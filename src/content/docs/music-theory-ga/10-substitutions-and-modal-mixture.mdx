## Diatonic substitution

### The idea

A **substitution** swaps one chord for another that does the same job. Wikipedia quotes Alfred Blatter: "A chord substitution occurs when a chord is replaced by another that is made to function like the original. Usually substituted chords possess two pitches in common with the triad that they are replacing." ([Chord substitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chord_substitution), citing Blatter 2007, p. 186). Lesson 9 showed which triads share two notes: those whose roots are a third apart. In C major, A minor and E minor can stand in for C, and D minor and A minor for F. Wikipedia calls the first case the relative major/minor substitution, which "shares two common tones" (same article).

The course takes the key's triads on the other degrees and ranks them by the notes they share with the chord ([`Lesson12.CourseSubstitutes`](https://github.com/spareilleux/learn/blob/aab1f4f/code/music-theory-ga/GaTheory/Lesson12.cs#L85-L98)). A seventh chord has four notes, so a triad can share three with it: B diminished is G7 without its root, and E minor is Cmaj7 without its own.

```text

chord      course                               GA                                   check
Am         C 2, F 2, Dm 1, Em 1                 C 2, F 2, Dm 1, Em 1                 ok
Em         C 2, G 2, Am 1, Bdim 1               C 2, G 2, Am 1, Bdim 1               ok

Cmaj7      Em 3, G 2, Am 2, F 1, Bdim 1         Em 3, G 2, Am 2, F 1, Bdim 1         ok

  the course's Bm7b5: B D F A; the closure's: B D F# A
```

Ties keep the order of the scale. The last row is a half-diminished seventh, B D F A, and D minor, D F A, shares three of its notes.

## Tritone substitution

### The idea

Open Music Theory: "Tritone substitutions replace a dominant chord with another dominant chord a tritone away. The name refers both to the interval between the original and substituted chords and to the fact that the two chords share the same tritone." ([Open Music Theory, "Substitutions"](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/substitutions/)). G7 is G B D F; D♭7 is D♭ F A♭ C♭, and C♭ sounds as B. The tritone B–F is in both chords with the roles swapped: B is the third of G7 and the seventh of D♭7. Both resolve to C, and the substitute does it by a half step in the bass: "the most reliable way to recognize a tritone substitution in a song is by its chromatic resolution downward by a minor second" (same chapter). The Streeling module [MUS-005 · Jazz Harmony for Guitar](../../streeling/music/mus-005-jazz-harmony/) works through it in section 5, "Substitution Techniques".

The course calls a chord dominant when it has a major third and a minor seventh above its root, with or without extensions, and names the substitute a diminished fifth above the root: D♭ for G.

## Secondary and backdoor dominants

### The idea

A **secondary dominant** is the dominant of a chord other than the tonic. Open Music Theory calls these "secondary dominant chords (V(7))" and gives the commonest: "ii becomes II♯, which is V/V" ([Open Music Theory, "Tonicization"](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/tonicization/)). In C major, that is D7 going to G. The target must be a major or minor triad: "Diminished and augmented triads can't be tonicized, since there are no diminished or augmented keys" (same chapter). [MUS-003 · How Harmony Works](../../streeling/music/mus-003-functional-harmony/) covers them in section 5; one row of its G major table is wrong (see the [journal](../journal/)).

The **backdoor dominant** reaches the tonic from a whole step below: ♭VII⁷ → I, B♭7 → C in C major. Wikipedia quotes Jerry Coker: "The I chord, in a given progression, is often preceded by IV–7 to ♭VII7, instead of the usual V7 chord" ([Chord substitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chord_substitution), citing Coker 1997, p. 82). MUS-005 has it as "Backdoor Dominant (bVII7 → I)".

The course reads a pair of chords with the five names GA's skill uses ([`Lesson12.CourseRelations`](https://github.com/spareilleux/learn/blob/aab1f4f/code/music-theory-ga/GaTheory/Lesson12.cs#L420-L437)): two dominant sevenths a tritone apart; a major triad or dominant seventh a fifth above a major or minor chord; a dominant seventh a whole step below its resolution; the same prime form, lesson 4's set class; and interval-class vectors at most 2 apart.

## Ranking candidates by interval-class vector

### The idea

Given one chord, the skill lists five substitutes "ranked by ICV distance" ([`ChordSubstitutionSkill.cs#L182`](https://github.com/GuitarAlchemist/ga/blob/a826864f3a012cad88e415954bf57eca0ce12aa6/Common/GA.Business.ML/Agents/Skills/ChordSubstitutionSkill.cs#L182)). GA's `FindNearby` ([`GrothendieckService.cs#L45-L90`](https://github.com/GuitarAlchemist/ga/blob/a826864f3a012cad88e415954bf57eca0ce12aa6/Common/GA.Domain.Services/Atonal/Grothendieck/GrothendieckService.cs#L45-L90)) walks all 4,096 pitch-class sets and keeps those whose interval-class vector ([lesson 4](../04-set-classes/)) differs from the chord's by at most 3, summed over the six classes. Each gets a cost of 0.6 times that distance ([`#L39-L42`](https://github.com/GuitarAlchemist/ga/blob/a826864f3a012cad88e415954bf57eca0ce12aa6/Common/GA.Domain.Services/Atonal/Grothendieck/GrothendieckService.cs#L39-L42)). The skill keeps the sets of the chord's size and takes the first five by cost ([`ChordSubstitutionSkill.cs#L164-L174`](https://github.com/GuitarAlchemist/ga/blob/a826864f3a012cad88e415954bf57eca0ce12aa6/Common/GA.Business.ML/Agents/Skills/ChordSubstitutionSkill.cs#L164-L174)).

Sorting by a key that ties is a C# question. [`Enumerable.OrderBy`](https://learn.microsoft.com/dotnet/api/system.linq.enumerable.orderby) is stable: "if the keys of two elements are equal, the order of the elements is preserved". Here nearly everything ties. A chord's transpositions and inversions have the same vector, so the 24 major and minor triads share one, and the 12 dominant and 12 half-diminished sevenths another. With the zero turned into a one, all of them come out at distance 1 and cost 0.60, and the stable sort leaves them in the order `FindNearby` met them: 12-bit order.

Two tools called `ga_chord_substitutions` therefore answer differently: the MCP server's ranks a key's chords by common tones, GaApi's ranks every set by vector distance.

## Modal mixture

### The idea

Open Music Theory: "Modal mixture (or borrowing) is the harmonic technique of mixing the notes from the parallel major and natural-minor modes (e.g., C major and C minor)." Mixture "is more common in major keys (borrowing from the parallel minor)", and it "changes the quality of a chord, but not its function" ([Open Music Theory, "Modal Mixture"](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/modal-mixture/)). Lowered roots get a flat: "Chords with lowered roots use a flat sign in front of the Roman numeral (e.g., ♭VI)." In C major the common ones are iv (F minor), ii° and iiø⁷ (D F A♭, with C), ♭VI (A♭), ♭III (E♭) and ♭VII (B♭); changing I to i is common too, V to v less so (same chapter).

Borrowing from the other parallel modes, Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian, Mixolydian and Locrian, extends the idea; jazz and rock musicians call it modal interchange. The course spells each mode on C, one letter per degree, and lists the triads C major does not have, with numerals read against the major scale ([`Lesson12.CourseBorrowed`](https://github.com/spareilleux/learn/blob/aab1f4f/code/music-theory-ga/GaTheory/Lesson12.cs#L499-L505), [`Lesson12.Numeral`](https://github.com/spareilleux/learn/blob/aab1f4f/code/music-theory-ga/GaTheory/Lesson12.cs#L58-L79)):

```text
== The course's borrowed chords, mode by mode: every triad of the parallel mode that C major lacks
mode         chords
Dorian       i Cm, bIII Eb, v Gm, vi° Adim, bVII Bb
Phrygian     i Cm, bII Db, bIII Eb, iv Fm, v° Gdim, bVI Ab, bvii Bbm
Lydian       II D, #iv° F#dim, vii Bm
Mixolydian   iii° Edim, v Gm, bVII Bb
Aeolian      i Cm, ii° Ddim, bIII Eb, iv Fm, v Gm, bVI Ab, bVII Bb
Locrian      i° Cdim, bII Db, biii Ebm, iv Fm, bV Gb, bVI Ab, bvii Bbm
count              course   GA       check
distinct chords    18       18       ok
```

The Aeolian row is Open Music Theory's mixture. Across the six modes there are 18 different chords; the GA column of the last row comes from the next section.

## Key takeaways

- A substitute does the original chord's job; a diatonic substitute shares two notes with a triad, its root a third away. GA's MCP closure ranks a key's chords the same way, but without a key it uses the major key of the root's letter, and at `a826864` it dropped alterations.
- Two dominant sevenths a tritone apart share their tritone, and the substitute resolves down a half step. GA's test for a major third and a minor seventh missed ninths and thirteenths, which did not carry their seventh at `a826864`.
- A secondary dominant is a major triad or dominant seventh a fifth above a major or minor chord. GA's skill checks the roots only, and its interval-vector distance turns 0 into 1.
- Ranking by vector distance ties every chord of a type, and LINQ's stable `OrderBy` keeps the enumeration order: the chatbot's five substitutes are the smallest 12-bit numbers of the chord's set class, Dm7♭5 first for every dominant seventh. A ranking needs a tie-breaker that means something.
- Modal mixture borrows from the parallel minor, and by extension from every parallel mode. GA's service finds the same 18 chords as the course for C major, but spells them with sharps, drops the ♭ from the numerals and credits each chord to the first mode of its loop. `ModalInterchange.yaml` has 4 of 13 chords and 3 of 6 numerals wrong, and its text reaches GA's YAML knowledge loader, though nothing at `a826864` runs the service that would store it with embeddings.
