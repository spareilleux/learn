[Open the 3D observatory](../demos/observatory/) in a browser with WebGL 2. The first load downloads about 60 MB of engine and scene data. On a phone or tablet, touch controls appear; they were tested in iPhone and iPad emulations. On an Android phone, the author found the lift hard to use without buttons of its own; it now has them, tested in phone-sized windows, not yet on a real device (*to verify*).

Click the scene to look around. Move with **WASD** (or **ZQSD**); hold **Shift** to sprint, press **Space** to jump and **Ctrl** or **C** to crouch (while sprinting, a slide); **Caps Lock** switches to a walk. Hold **Q** or **E** (**A** or **E** on an AZERTY keyboard), as in *Return to Castle Wolfenstein*, or **Alt** with **A/D** (or **Q/D**), to lean round a corner; **Alt** with **W/S** (or **Z/S**) rises on your toes or ducks. Near the lift, **E** is the lift's. Aimed at a course book, lean the way its title reads (right; in French, left) and you tilt your head to read the spine: the view turns to it and closes in until the title runs level across the screen. Reading, the mouse takes you along the shelf from spine to spine, the head still tilted: it moves your gaze the way it moves on the tilted screen; **A/D** (or **Q/D**) and the wheel step one spine at a time, and a click opens the one you read. **Ctrl+W** closes the tab in most browsers, so **C** is the safer crouch. The steel box, the piers, the statue's pedestal, the shelves and the galleries' rail stop you. **Esc** releases the mouse. If your browser does not grant pointer lock, hold the right mouse button and drag to look. Press **1–9** for camera viewpoints: the camera flies there in 1.4 to 2.4 seconds, rising and turning on the way. **8** climbs to the stained glass in the drum; **9** cuts to the roof, outside, on the drum's rim; walk, and you step down onto the terrace, where the lift's kiosk is a walk away. **1–8** or **Back inside** bring you in again. Press **F** for free flight, then **E/Space** to rise and **C** to descend. If your system asks for reduced motion, the viewpoints change at once, and the view no longer widens, tilts or dips when you sprint, slide or land; reading a spine, which you hold, still tilts and closes in. Mouse capture was verified in the native build, but its behavior in ordinary Chrome, Edge and Firefox windows still needs testing; please report browser-specific problems.

A lift climbs to the two galleries. It stands behind you when you arrive, in the bay facing the steel box's door: a brass cage panelled in walnut under a gilt-crested dome, with a ring of green light under its roof, between columns of dark bronze. Over every landing, a dial's needle follows the cage, and the sheave at the top turns as it rides. Its gates fold like an accordion; the motor hums while the cage rides, and a bell rings at every arrival. Walk in, then press **Space** to go up a floor, **Ctrl** or **C** to go down, or **E** (up, or back down from the top); on a touch screen, **▲** and **▼**. At a landing, **E** calls the cage, and so does walking against its closed gate. Walk out onto a gallery and round it, along the shelves; a rail edges it on the hall's side. At the rail, facing the hall, **Space** vaults it: you drop to the hall and land unhurt, even from the upper gallery. The lift also goes on to the roof. Past the upper gallery, the cage rises through the ceiling, where the view dips to black, into a small kiosk of bronze and glass on the lead roof. Its door faces the drum: walk out onto the roof, between the drum and a balustrade at the eave, under the galactic core, and come back down by the cage, which **E** (on a touch screen, **Call**) calls from beside the kiosk. The roof was tested with simulated keys in both renderers, also at 10 frames a second and with a stalled frame, not yet in a browser (*to verify*). The lift, the lean and the new keys were tested with simulated keys in both renderers, not yet by hand in a browser, where **Alt** may also reach the browser's menu (*to verify*).

On a touch screen, the stick at the bottom left walks, and sprints past its rim; a drag anywhere else looks around. **▲** jumps and **▼** crouches, and in the lift they go up and down; **Fly** switches to free flight, where they rise and descend; **Views** steps through the nine viewpoints; **Courses** opens the catalogue. Near the lift, **Call** brings the cage to your landing; in the cage, a button for each landing sends it there. Tap a book for its card, and again to open the course. At a tapped book, two leaning figures appear next to the arrows: hold the one that leans the way its title reads to read the spine. While you hold it, a drag goes along the shelf from spine to spine, and a tap on another spine reads that one. The help at the top goes a few seconds after your first touch, and while a book's card shows; **?** brings it back, and a tap on it puts it away. The lean and the reading were tested with simulated keys and touches, also at 10 frames a second, and the help with simulated touches on a phone-sized screen, not yet in a browser (*to verify*). On a phone held upright the view is widened: on an iPhone, 50 degrees across instead of 32.

Hold **Tab** for the curio wheel, a library's pastiche of the weapon wheels in shooters such as *Wolfenstein*: time slows to a quarter, the pointer picks a curio round the screen's centre, and letting go of **Tab** puts it in your hand; a click uses it. A quick tap of **Tab** leaves the wheel open instead, until you pick a curio or press **Tab** or **Esc** again; on a computer, the round button at the bottom left, which shows the curio in your hand, opens it with a click. Bare hands open books as before. The Index, a white glove, points at things and names them in gilt: rude, but informative. The monocle, held, brings everything two and a half times closer inside its brass rim. The banker's lamp, its cord plugged into nothing, lights the way. The wooden wand knows one spell: out of a puff of gilt where it lands steps a small TARS, four slabs on a hinge after the robot of the film *Interstellar*, and walks off as the film's does, two slabs planted while the other two swing round them; eight at most. The rubber duck is there to hear your code explained, and to be thrown when it disagrees: it bounces, squeaks and stays where it lands. The ankh brings the ducks back to life, and they waddle home to your feet; the TARS walk back to you. On a touch screen, the **Curios** button above the stick, which shows the curio in your hand, opens the wheel; a tap on a slot takes that curio, and a tap on the scene uses it there; the monocle and the lamp stay on until a second tap. Every model and every sound is made by the scene's own code. The wheel was tested with simulated keys and touches in both renderers, also at 10 frames a second, and in headless Chrome, where holding or tapping **Tab** opens it and the focus stays on the scene; other browsers are *to verify*.

The eighth curio is a pocket drone, nine centimetres across on four rotors. Use it, and it rises from your hand, then the view cuts to it as it tours the observatory on its own, lap after lap, in about four minutes: low round the steel box, up to Seldon's face, through a bay into each gallery along the books, up round the drum past the stained glass, out through the oculus, wide over the domes of Trantor and along the drum's lit windows, then back in and down across the hall. It flies as a camera drone does: it slows before each bend to a speed it can hold there, leans into it, never more than 25 degrees, and turns its nose at a bounded rate; outside, the air stirs it a little more. The view follows it from behind; **V** or **On board** puts you on board, on a gimbal that keeps the horizon level. **Esc** or **Land** brings it back to your hand, where you stood: while it flies, you stay put. In the multiverse corridor it stays in your hand, since there is no sky there. Its route was checked against every surface of the hall and the roof: it keeps 35 cm from all of them, and the craft, over a simulated lap, 12 cm. The drone was tested with simulated keys and touches in both renderers, and in headless Chrome on an emulated phone; a real browser on a computer, and real phones, are *to verify*.

Every course of this site is shelved in the library, among the other books. On the ground floor each area of the site has its bay, marked by a banner in its colour hanging under the gallery, with the area's name in gilt. At eye level, under a lacquered sign that names the area and counts its courses, a brass lamp lights them: a series in one binding between two brass bookends, whose spines carry the real titles. Streeling University has a book for each of its courses, ranged by department: each department in its own cloth, a brass bookend between two departments, and a card under its books with its code (AUD, MAT…); a course's card names its department and its number. Aim at a spine, with the crosshair while the mouse is captured or with the pointer: the book slides out and its card appears. Click it or press **Enter** to open the course in a new tab. Press **L** for a catalogue of every course. The scene reads the course list from the site when it starts, so a new course reaches the shelves without a new build. The **EN / FR / ES** selector at the top right, or **T**, switches the titles, signs, cards, catalogue and course links. Under it, the scene shows its version, when it was built, and its age in days:hours:minutes. English is the default unless you came from a French or Spanish page, and the page's address keeps your choice. The keyboard help stays in English.

In the hall, in front of the Streeling University bay, a marble statue of Hari Seldon faces the centre from a dark pedestal lettered in gilt with his dates in the Galactic Era; the sphere in his hands glows faintly. Hold your look on it for a second, or press **H**, click it or tap it: a hologram of one of [IX](../machine-learning-ix/)'s pipelines unfolds in a spiral round the statue and replays, step by step in its run order, a real run on the author's machine, each panel charting its step's result. Each opening plays the next of five pipelines, and **H** closes it. It was tested with a simulated look in both renderers and with **H** in headless Chrome; a tap on a real phone is *to verify*. It is heard too, in sounds the scene synthesises itself: a glide as it opens and closes, and a note at each step; by ear, they are *to verify*. The first time you come within six metres of the statue with the sphere in view, the hologram opens by itself, once, so that it is not found only by chance, and the keyboard help's third line names **H**, **R** and the wand's TARS; both were tested with a simulated approach in Godot and in headless Chrome, and whether a first visitor finds the TARS from that line is *to verify*. Hold your look on the gilt inscription of his pedestal, or press **R**, click it or tap it, and his recording plays, as in Asimov's *Foundation*, where his recorded image appears at the appointed hour: dust gathers beside the pedestal and climbs into a life-size Seldon made of points of light, one for each vertex of the statue's own figure, so that no actor's face enters the scene. He turns to you, three lines written for this scene show over his head, and he lifts away as dust; **R** sends him away sooner. It was tested with a simulated look, keys and taps in both renderers and at the lowest quality level; its sound, and a tap on a real phone, are *to verify*. The capitals, the bases of the piers, the arches, the entablature and the ribs of the ceiling are picked out in gilt, and the floor is paved in rings of pale and dark marble around a compass rose whose points reach out from under the steel box. The light of the galactic core, high over the oculus, casts the shadows of the box, the piers and the statue across the paving, and the marble darkens where they meet the floor. Over each bay, on the entablature, a mosaic panel gathers itself out of drifting gold dust when you hold your look on it for a second, click it or tap it, and goes back to dust half a minute after you look away: the author asked for frescoes of nanoparticles like those of the *Foundation* TV series. The sixteen panels, galaxies, books and instruments in the manner of a Byzantine gold mosaic, were drawn locally by Z-Image-Turbo and contain no figure, lettering or image from the series. On a weak GPU the idle dust goes and fewer particles fly. The frieze was tested with a simulated look, clicks and taps in both renderers and at the lowest quality level, and with a click in headless Chrome; a tap on a real phone is *to verify*.

The sky over the oculus is Trantor's, not Earth's. Trantor lies near the centre of the Galaxy, so its core stands high and large, and stars crowd towards it. Around the core, the Galaxy's bulge and dust lanes are an image drawn locally by Z-Image-Turbo; beyond it, the Milky Way is ESO's panorama by Serge Brunier, re-projected as if seen from nearer the centre. Both are an interpretation, not a simulation: the panorama's stars and dust lanes are still those seen from Earth. Under it, the orrery is brass and ivory. From the roof (**9**), the domes of Trantor's surface stretch to the horizon under the core's light, and the drum is dressed limestone banded with brushed steel: Blender modelled the courses, chamfers, joints, bolted bands and slotted plates, and SDXL 1.0 drew the grain of the stone and of the steel. Its cost on a phone is not measured yet (*to verify*).

Double-click the steel box (double-tap it on a touch screen) to step into the multiverse corridor: an endless row of numbered steel doors under faint ceiling lamps, lit by the lantern you carry, each glowing with the light of another world. The idea comes from Blake Crouch's novel *Dark Matter*; the corridor and its twelve worlds are shaders of our own. The **Leave** button, **Esc**, or the lit doorway behind you bring you back in front of the box.

This prototype contains a Blender-authored, textured steel portal and textures generated locally with ComfyUI. The marble, the grain of the book bindings, the tooled stone, the grime on the steel and three stained-glass panels (sun and moon, an elven tree, an alembic) come from cached SDXL 1.0; the pier ornament comes from SDXL 1.0 with cached ControlNet Union SDXL. Shaders turn them into leather and cloth bindings with raised bands and gilt rules, dressed stone with joints and coffers, brushed steel and backlit stained glass. The balustrade has turned bronze balusters under a walnut handrail. The statue was generated locally too: Z-Image-Turbo drew an invented elderly scholar, not the likeness of any actor, and TRELLIS.2 turned the image into a mesh, remeshed in ComfyUI and normalised in Blender; a shader gives it white marble.

The source scene's richer desktop rendering uses Godot Forward+ features, including screen-space reflections, indirect light, volumetric fog and glow. The browser build uses Godot's WebGL 2 Compatibility renderer, so those desktop-only effects are reduced or absent: the stained glass throws no shafts of light there, and the books, bronze and steel get simple stand-ins for the missing indirect light. For anti-aliasing, a computer renders the scene 1.5 times finer than the screen and scales it down, and a phone or tablet uses 4x multisampling; each steps down if frames get slow. If frames still come slower than about 36 a second, the scene lowers its own quality in steps and says so at the bottom of the screen: anti-aliasing first, then the glow and the gilt ornament, then a lower resolution with simpler books and balusters, then the shadows and the far lamps. Add `?quality=0` to the address to keep everything, or `?quality=1` to `5` for a fixed level. On a first visit, while the browser compiles the scene's shaders, frames come slow for a few seconds; if the scene stepped down then, it steps back up once when frames come fast again. In Chrome's software renderer, standing in for the weakest GPU, a frame went from 1.5 to 0.4 seconds; on real weak GPUs it is *to verify*. Their cost on a real phone is not measured yet (*to verify*). Neither build claims real-time hardware ray tracing; Blender Cycles baked the steel textures, but does not render the interactive scene.

The room is still stylized, especially its central beam, and the lettering on the book spines is decorative glyphs, not readable titles; only the courses' spines carry real ones. This is a playable prototype, not a finished photorealistic environment. The [Blender course](../blender/) and [ComfyUI course](../comfyui/) explain the tools behind the asset pipeline.
